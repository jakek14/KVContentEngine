## Title

When $70 Buys One Lead: The New Math of B2B Customer Acquisition

## Subtitle

Google Ads CPL hit $70.11 in 2026. Your acquisition playbook was written for a different number.

## Post

There's a number that's been bothering me.

$70.11.

That's the average cost per lead on Google Ads for B2B SaaS in 2026. Not an outlier. Not a niche vertical. The average.

Three years ago that number was around $43. CAC across B2B categories is up 40 to 60% since 2023. The average cost-per-click for B2B SaaS is up 57% versus its eight-year benchmark. And the median CAC payback period -- how long it takes to recover what you spent acquiring a customer -- has stretched from 11 months in 2021 to 18 months in 2026.

The story most people tell about this is "paid channels are getting more expensive." That's true but it misses the deeper problem.

---

**The cost went up. The playbook didn't change.**

The standard B2B SaaS go-to-market was built for a world where you could spend $20 on a lead, close 10 to 15% of them, and have the math work out. Spend on paid, generate leads, hand to sales, book meetings, close deals, repeat.

When a lead costs $70, that math doesn't hold. You need either much higher close rates, much higher deal sizes, or a fundamentally different model for how you fill the top of the funnel.

Most companies have responded to rising CAC by spending more on acquisition. Which is the financial equivalent of buying more tickets to a lottery that's started paying out less.

The instinct makes sense. Sales teams know how to work a pipeline. Marketing knows how to run campaigns. When you need more revenue, you turn up the dial on the thing that worked before.

The problem is that "the thing that worked before" was calibrated for a completely different cost structure.

---

**The math that actually changes everything**

Top-quartile B2B SaaS companies trade at 24x revenue. Bottom quartile: 5x.

Same ARR. Nearly five times the valuation.

The difference isn't growth rate. It's net revenue retention. It's whether existing customers expand.

A company with 120% NRR doesn't need to acquire as many new customers to grow at the same rate as a company with 90% NRR. Every dollar spent on acquisition generates more than one dollar of ARR over time because the denominator keeps growing without additional spend.

The company at 90% NRR is on a treadmill. Every new dollar acquired partially replaces what's walking out the door.

Rising CAC is a much more urgent problem for companies with low NRR than for companies where expansion carries the load. That's not a reason to ignore acquisition costs. It's a reason to build retention into the acquisition math before the first sale.

One of the first questions I'd ask any founder looking at a $70 CPL: what's your NRR? Because if it's below 100%, the acquisition problem is actually a retention problem that's become visible at scale.

---

**The only channel that got cheaper**

While paid channels climbed 40 to 60%, one growth channel's cost hasn't moved: word of mouth.

Peer referrals, community mentions, earned reputation -- these cost essentially nothing to generate if you have the product and customer relationships to back them up. And in 2026, the evidence for their impact is sharper than ever.

B2B buyers complete 70 to 80% of their purchase journey before the first sales contact. During that research phase, they're asking peers in private Slack groups, reading community threads, querying AI assistants about which tools to use. None of that fires a pixel. None of it shows up in your attribution report.

But it shapes who they reach out to and what they already believe when they do.

The companies with strong word of mouth don't just have lower CAC -- they have faster sales cycles, higher close rates, and better retention. Customers who arrived through trusted referrals already have accurate expectations. They didn't get oversold into the product. They came in having heard from someone they trust that it was worth their time.

Building for word of mouth is a longer game than turning up a Google Ads budget. It requires being genuinely good, being genuinely visible in the spaces where your customers talk, and building something people actually want to recommend.

But in a world where a paid lead costs $70, it's the only growth lever that gets cheaper the bigger you get.

---

**The traffic you already paid for**

Here's the thing most acquisition-focused teams miss: they're spending $70 to get a lead while their website converts 2 to 3% of visitors.

The other 97% leave without identifying themselves.

If you're spending to drive traffic -- paid, content, SEO, social -- and 97 of every 100 visitors disappear without any signal, you've paid for intent data that immediately evaporates.

The companies extracting the most from their existing traffic are the ones treating every visit as signal, not just the ones that convert to form fills. Who landed on the pricing page twice this week? Who spent eight minutes reading the case study? Who returned three times via direct and left without converting?

Those are real buying signals. High-intent signals. In a world where a new lead costs $70, ignoring the behavioral data on visitors who didn't fill out a form is an expensive habit.

The acquisition math doesn't just change with which channels you use. It changes with how much value you extract per visitor once they arrive.

---

**What the playbook looks like when you rebuild it**

Obsess over CAC payback, not just CAC. A high payback number is often an NRR problem in disguise. The companies holding median payback under 12 months are doing it through a combination of lower acquisition cost and higher expansion revenue -- both matter. Fix the retention side first.

Build for word of mouth from day one -- not as a separate program, as a product design principle. Would your best customer tell a peer about this unprompted? If not, ask why before running another campaign.

Treat your website as a pipeline asset, not a brochure. Every visitor is a potential signal. The ones who arrive, look at something that indicates intent, and leave are not lost. They're just invisible -- and that's solvable.

Stop treating "more budget" as the default response to rising CAC. The reflex is understandable. The economics increasingly don't support it.

---

**The bigger picture**

The playbooks that built B2B SaaS companies from 2019 to 2022 were calibrated for cheap paid distribution, abundant third-party data, and short attribution cycles. All three have changed significantly.

The companies that compound over the next five years won't necessarily be the ones that spend the most on acquisition. They'll be the ones that built a product customers expand in, a reputation customers talk about, and a system for capturing the intent signals their traffic already generates.

$70 a lead isn't a crisis. It's a forcing function.

The forcing function is: your marketing has to be worth more per dollar than it was three years ago.

The companies figuring that out now will have a compounding advantage over the ones still running a playbook designed for a number that no longer exists.
