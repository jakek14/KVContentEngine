## Title

The Self-Serve SaaS Math Nobody Wants to Do

## Subtitle

At 10% monthly churn, you lose your entire customer base in under a year. Most founders don't run this math until it's too late.

## Post

There's a number most self-serve SaaS founders don't compute until they're staring at a flat MRR line wondering what went wrong.

It's not CAC. It's not LTV. It's the compounding effect of monthly churn.

The industry average for self-serve SaaS is 8-10% monthly churn. That sounds bad but manageable -- a number you know about, acknowledge, and plan to improve. Here's what it actually means: at 10% monthly churn, you lose roughly 72% of your customer base every year. At 8%, it's closer to 63%.

Run that math on your own product.

If you're adding 15% new customers every month but churning 10%, your net growth is 5%. In absolute terms. Monthly. Which sounds fine until you realize you're burning tremendous energy and spend just to stay roughly where you are, minus a bit.

The treadmill doesn't feel like a treadmill until you've been on it long enough to notice you're tired.

**Why founders miss this**

The problem isn't the math. Anyone can do the math. The problem is timing.

When you're early, churn is hidden behind growth. You add 20 customers, lose 2, net 18 -- who's focused on the 2? As the base gets larger, that 10% becomes a larger and larger absolute number. 10% of 100 customers is 10. 10% of 1,000 is 100. You're still adding customers at 15% per month, but now you need to add 100 just to stay flat, 150 to show any net growth.

And somewhere around here is when founders start asking why growth slowed down when they didn't change anything.

What changed is the math finally caught up.

**The comparison that puts it in perspective**

Enterprise SaaS churn runs 1-2% monthly. That's the benchmark for companies with longer contracts, dedicated onboarding, a customer success team, and purchasing decisions made by someone who signs off on a 12-month commitment.

Self-serve is the opposite: low friction in, low friction out. The buyer who signed up with a credit card because your landing page was good will churn the moment the product becomes confusing, the use case doesn't land, or they forget they subscribed.

Enterprise companies lose 11-21% of their customer base annually.

Self-serve companies lose 63-72%.

Both can grow. But they operate on completely different retention dynamics, and a self-serve founder who benchmarks against enterprise churn numbers is building on a false floor.

**What actually moves self-serve churn**

The good news: self-serve churn is more responsive to product decisions than enterprise churn. You can move it. The bad news: most interventions that work are slow.

The ones I've seen matter most:

Time to value. If someone signs up and doesn't get a clear "oh, this is what it does for me" moment within their first session, they're probably not coming back for a second. Self-serve products often have beautiful onboarding that explains how to use features without ever delivering the core promise. The fastest path to lower churn is closing the gap between signup and first meaningful result.

The right customers. Self-serve products often attract a long tail of marginal-fit users because the barrier to try is low. Someone who signed up because your headline was compelling but your product doesn't actually solve their problem will churn no matter how good your product is. Higher self-serve churn is sometimes a signal that acquisition is pulling in the wrong ICP, not that the product is broken.

Notification and re-engagement. Self-serve customers don't have an account executive checking in. They have whatever you send them. The best self-serve products build habit loops -- notifications that bring users back when something relevant happens, not just welcome sequences that fire and go quiet.

Usage-based intervention. Churn almost always shows up in usage data before it shows up in cancellation. Customers who haven't logged in for 14 days, haven't completed setup, or are only using one feature are at higher risk. Catching them before they cancel costs a fraction of what it costs to win them back after.

**The metric to anchor on**

If monthly churn isn't in your weekly review, it should be. Not just as a number to track -- as a leading indicator that tells you whether the product is delivering on what the landing page promised.

The founders I know who've broken through to real self-serve scale aren't the ones who nailed acquisition. They're the ones who figured out why their customers stayed -- and then went all-in on creating more of those conditions.

The leaky bucket metaphor gets overused. But there's a version of it that's actually useful: before you invest more in the pump, check how fast the bucket drains.

Most founders I talk to are focused on filling it faster.

A smaller number are fixing the holes.
