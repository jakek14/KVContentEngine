## Title

The Numbers Were Always Wrong. Now You Can See It.

## Subtitle

Meta just made their conversion data honest. Google rewrote their ad contract. What both changes are really telling you about the state of marketing measurement in 2026.

## Post

Two things happened in marketing this week that most founders probably didn't notice.

Meta quietly changed how they count conversions. Google rewrote their Ads Terms of Service for the first time since 2018.

On the surface, these look like platform housekeeping. Legal updates. Attribution tweaks. The kind of thing you forward to your marketing team and move on.

They're not. Both changes are telling you the same thing: the data you've been running your marketing strategy on was always less reliable than the dashboards suggested. And the people who built those dashboards knew it.

---

**The Meta attribution change**

For years, Meta's default attribution model included likes, saves, shares, and comments in your conversion reporting. Not just real link clicks -- engagement of any kind got bundled into the number.

This created a problem that was easy to ignore. If someone liked your ad and kept scrolling, they showed up in your data as a potential conversion. Your ROAS looked better than it was. Your campaigns looked more effective than they were. And every time you went to justify Meta spend in a budget conversation, you were leaning on a number that had been quietly inflated.

Meta's 2026 update changed that. Click-through attribution now counts only real link clicks. Likes, saves, shares, and comments are tracked separately under a new "Engage-through attribution" category that is not bundled into your conversion totals.

A lot of advertisers are seeing their Meta numbers drop. Some are panicking. Others are asking their agencies why performance suddenly looks worse.

Here is the honest answer: it didn't get worse. You can finally see what it actually was.

The companies winning on Meta over the next six months are the ones who treat this as a reset. The campaigns that hold up under accurate attribution are the real ones. The ones that fall apart were never working -- you just couldn't tell.

---

**The Google ToS rewrite**

Google's update is different but related. The new Terms of Service -- effective July 1, 2026, the first update since 2018 -- make explicit something that was always true but never formally acknowledged: Google uses your ad performance data, your creative, and your conversion signals to train their AI systems.

Smart Bidding, Performance Max, Demand Gen. These tools run on the signals every advertiser produces. The new ToS makes that usage contractual rather than implied.

This shouldn't be shocking. We all understood, at some level, that the data flowing through these platforms was shaping the models we were bidding against. But having it explicit changes the strategic calculus.

The most important implication is not that Google is stealing your data. It's that the platforms you depend on have a structural incentive to optimize for their own performance metrics, which are not always the same as your business metrics.

Google optimizes for "conversions" as they define them. If your definition of a conversion doesn't perfectly align with theirs, your campaigns optimize for the wrong thing. The new ToS is a reminder that you don't control those definitions -- and that you should be running your own measurement layer alongside whatever the platform reports.

---

**What both changes are really saying**

Marketers have always known, at some level, that platform reporting is optimistic. The dashboards are built by the people selling the advertising. They are not built by people who have a financial interest in showing you that your campaigns underperformed.

This isn't a cynical observation. It's just a structural reality of the industry. Every platform has some incentive to make their product look effective. That doesn't mean the platforms are lying -- it means that what they optimize to show you is always colored by what keeps you spending.

What's unusual about this moment is that Meta just voluntarily made their numbers look worse. They didn't have to change this. They could have kept bundling engagement into conversion totals. Advertisers were not rising up in protest. The change made the platform look less effective in the short term.

The reason they did it matters: third-party measurement was catching the discrepancy. Advertisers running independent attribution tools were seeing the gap between what Meta reported and what was actually driving revenue. As external measurement gets more sophisticated, platforms that optimize for honest reporting will win over the ones that paper over the gap.

---

**What to do with this**

First: update your baselines. If you're running Meta ads, your "real" conversion rate is probably the one you're seeing now after the attribution change, not the one you were seeing last quarter. Reset your benchmarks. Don't compare to a number that was artificially inflated.

Second: build your own measurement layer. Platform attribution is one input, not the whole picture. First-party data -- from your CRM, your email platform, your actual revenue -- is the only measurement you fully control. If you're making significant budget decisions based purely on what Meta or Google report, you're building on someone else's foundation.

Third: stop being surprised when platforms change the rules. This is always how it works. Your distribution channels are rented. The terms will change. The algorithms will shift. The attribution models will evolve. The companies that handle this best are the ones who've built enough owned data and owned audience that no single platform change can blow up their marketing program.

The second half of 2026 starts today. The companies that go into it with honest baselines and independent measurement will make better decisions than the ones still running on the old numbers.

Reset the baseline. Know what you actually know. Go from there.
