## Title

The Whole Point Was Wrong

## Subtitle

The companies still standing in 2026 didn't out-grow everyone else. They out-retained them.

## Post

I want to tell you about a number that I can't stop thinking about.

60%.

That's the share of D2C companies that raised a Series A between 2019 and 2021 that are either gone or currently trying to raise a down round. Not 20%. Not the bottom quartile of underperformers. Sixty percent of everyone who raised at those peak valuations.

These weren't bad founders. Most of them were running real businesses with real demand. They raised from credible investors. They had growth. They had press. Some of them had viral moments, strong LTVs on paper, and customer logos that made their deck look good.

What they didn't have was retention.

---

### The mistake wasn't spending on growth

The playbook during 2019-2021 was clear: raise capital, spend aggressively on acquisition, show the growth curve, raise more. Nobody was getting penalized for burning on CAC. In fact, if you weren't burning, you were considered timid.

The problem with that playbook is that it lets you hide a fundamentally broken business for a surprisingly long time. If you're adding enough new customers every month, churn looks like a rounding error. The growth covers it. The deck looks clean. The metrics show a hockey stick.

Then the capital gets tighter. You stop adding as aggressively. And suddenly what was a rounding error turns into a hole that nobody can plug.

The best way I've heard it described: growth is a drug that masks churn. The moment you stop using the drug, the churn you were ignoring becomes the whole story.

---

### What investors actually look at now

The shift that's happened over the past 18 months is that the investors who survived the correction learned to look through the growth curve and straight at the cohorts.

What do customers who signed up six months ago look like today? Not in aggregate -- by cohort. Are the month-3 customers behaving differently from month-1? Is there a meaningful difference between customers who activated quickly and those who didn't?

A brand with a 40% Year 1 repeat purchase rate has fundamentally different economics than one at 15%. The former recovers its CAC faster, generates compounding revenue from an existing base, and doesn't need to keep buying attention just to hold revenue flat. The latter is trapped -- every month they have to re-acquire their own revenue.

The number investors want to see before writing a check in 2026 isn't month-over-month growth. It's LTV:CAC at 3:1 or better. CAC payback under 6 months. Cohort retention that holds.

These aren't new metrics. They've always been the real ones. What changed is that investors stopped accepting growth as a substitute for them.

---

### The same pattern shows up in SaaS

It's not just D2C. The same lesson is playing out in B2B software.

The old conversion funnel for SaaS goes like this: run ads, capture leads, nurture them through email sequences, get them to a demo or trial, hope they convert. The marketing-qualified lead model.

MQLs convert at about 9%.

The companies that shifted to product-qualified leads -- people who have actually used the product and hit a meaningful activation milestone -- convert at 25%. The same funnel, different signal quality. Nearly 3x better conversion.

There's a parallel insight on trial length. Most SaaS founders I've talked to assume longer trials mean higher conversion -- more time to see the value. The data says the opposite. Seven-day trials convert at 40%. Sixty-day trials convert at 30%. The extra time doesn't create confidence. It creates procrastination.

What drives conversion isn't time. It's urgency paired with hitting the activation milestone. The moment a user gets genuine value from the product -- the first time the thing actually works for them -- that's the conversion event. Everything after it is just following through.

The companies building around this insight are making their onboarding sharper, not their trial windows longer. They're asking "what does our user need to experience to become a customer?" and then engineering the path to that experience as fast as possible.

---

### What I actually changed my mind about

For a while I believed the conventional wisdom: give users time, give them room to breathe, don't rush the sale. That felt respectful of the customer.

What I've come to believe instead: urgency is respectful when it's paired with genuine value. If you've built something that actually solves a real problem, the kindest thing you can do is help someone experience that solution as quickly as possible. Dragging out the process doesn't serve them. It just delays the moment of clarity.

The same logic applies to retention. The founders who over-invested in retention early -- who built support infrastructure, activation flows, and customer success motions before they had the headcount to justify them -- often looked inefficient from the outside. Why are they spending so much on existing customers when they could be spending on acquisition?

The answer became obvious over the past two years: because their customers stayed. And staying customers compound. New customers just restart the clock.

---

### The companies that figured this out

The companies I find most interesting right now are the ones that solved retention first and growth second. Not because they didn't want to grow -- but because they understood that retention is what makes growth compound instead of cancel out.

They're running tighter trial windows with sharper activation moments. They're measuring cohort retention instead of aggregate churn. They're building CS infrastructure before the headcount feels justified. And when they go to investors, they bring data that actually tells the story of a healthy business.

The ones who did it the other way -- growth first, retention later -- are now living in the results of that choice.

Sixty percent of them.
