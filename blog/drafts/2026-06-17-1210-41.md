## Title

The Seat Is Dead

## Subtitle

AI agents don't buy seats. They replace them. Here's what that means for your SaaS pricing -- and why most founders aren't moving fast enough.

## Post

For the last 20 years, SaaS pricing had one dominant logic: charge per seat.

More users = more revenue. Expansion meant adding headcount. Renewal math was simple -- count the active seats, send the invoice. The model was clean, predictable, and it worked.

In 2026, that logic is breaking.

Not slowly. Fast. And the founders who don't see it coming are going to have a bad time.

---

**What changed**

The catalyst is AI agents. Not AI features built into your software -- actual autonomous agents that do work.

Here's the problem the per-seat model wasn't built for: agents don't need a seat. They replace seats.

If a customer used to have 15 people using your project management software, and they can now run 80% of that workload with 3 people and 2 AI agents -- what happens to your seat count? It goes down. Your customer's efficiency went up. Your revenue went down. The product didn't get worse. The pricing model just became misaligned.

IDC forecasts that 70% of software vendors will refactor away from pure per-seat models by 2028. Chargebee's most recent data shows 43% of SaaS companies have already moved to hybrid pricing, with that number projected to hit 61% by the end of 2026.

This isn't coming. It's already here.

---

**The three models replacing it**

Per-seat isn't dying without successors. The industry is converging on three alternatives, and which one you pick matters more than you'd think.

**Usage-based**: you charge based on consumption -- API calls, messages sent, records processed, whatever the natural unit of value is for your product. Stripe does this. Twilio does this. The logic is clean: customers pay for what they use. Alignment between value delivered and price charged is tight.

The catch: revenue becomes unpredictable. Customers with low usage months pay almost nothing. Your FP&A team will not love you.

**Outcome-based**: you charge based on results. Revenue influenced. Time saved. Deals closed. Errors caught. The model that makes the most philosophical sense -- if your product delivers value, you get paid for value -- but it's the hardest to operationalize. How do you audit an outcome? What happens when the customer's results drop for reasons unrelated to your product?

Most companies experimenting with outcome-based pricing today are doing it in narrow, well-defined use cases where the measurement is clean. It's not a replacement for per-seat across the board -- it's a premium add-on in categories where the result is concrete and provable.

**Hybrid**: a base subscription (often per-seat or flat) with a usage or outcome component layered on. The pragmatic middle. You get recurring revenue predictability from the base. You capture upside from usage. 43% of the market is already here and growing.

For most B2B SaaS companies, hybrid is the direction of travel. The question is which components you combine and what your core value metric actually is.

---

**The real question most founders skip**

Before arguing about usage vs. outcome vs. hybrid, there's a more important question: what is the actual unit of value your product delivers?

Not "seats." Not "users." What does the customer get from your product that they couldn't easily get elsewhere? Where does value actually accumulate?

For some products it's data -- the more records processed, the more signals generated, the more value the customer has. For others it's workflow automation -- decisions made, tasks completed, errors avoided. For others it's access to a network or dataset that's unique to your platform.

The pricing model that compounds is the one where the customer's value grows as they use you more -- and where your revenue grows as their success grows. Per-seat made that alignment easy when every user was a human who needed a login. The alignment breaks when a single API call can do what 10 users used to do.

HubSpot's move from seat-based to contact-based pricing is the canonical example. The unit that correlated with customer value wasn't "number of users logged in." It was "number of contacts in the system." As customers grew their audiences, they got more value, and HubSpot captured more of it. Customers who weren't growing paid less and churned less resentfully. The alignment was better.

That move wasn't obvious before it worked. It required honest analysis of where value was actually accumulating.

---

**What the urgency actually is**

I want to be direct about timing, because there's a tendency to treat this as a problem for "eventually."

Your largest customers are already solving this for themselves. Procurement teams at enterprise companies have gotten much better at auditing software spend and negotiating contracts that eliminate seats they're not using. The customer who used to let 40 seats run because "it's easier than managing it" is now running a license audit before renewal.

Simultaneously, the AI tooling available to those customers is improving fast. The question of "how many people actually need to use this?" is getting answered more aggressively every quarter.

If your growth model depends on net new seats from existing customers, and your customers are simultaneously deploying AI agents to reduce the number of humans who need to touch anything -- you're facing headwinds that don't show up in your churn rate yet but will.

Gross revenue retention looks fine right now. Net revenue retention is where the pressure shows first.

---

**What I'd actually do**

If I were rebuilding a SaaS pricing model in 2026, here's where I'd start.

Figure out the real value metric first. Seats are a proxy for value. Find the actual unit. It's usually something that scales with customer success: contacts, records, API calls, outcomes, seats-but-weighted-by-usage-depth. Then test whether your current pricing tracks that metric or diverges from it.

Build a hybrid model with a floor. A base subscription that feels reasonable to any customer of your target size -- predictable, budget-friendly -- plus a usage component that captures upside as they scale. The floor gives you predictability. The usage layer gives you expansion without needing new seats.

Get the measurement infrastructure in place before the pricing model. You can't run usage-based or outcome-based pricing if you can't measure the unit accurately. This is where companies get stuck -- the model makes sense conceptually, but the instrumentation to bill on it doesn't exist yet.

And start the conversation with customers before you flip the model, not after. The companies that have navigated pricing transitions well have generally been transparent about where things are going 12-18 months before the change. Customers hate surprises on billing. They're much more tolerant of being prepared for change.

---

The per-seat era worked. It served SaaS well for two decades. The model isn't wrong -- it just doesn't fit the world anymore.

AI agents are doing work that used to require people. The pricing model that made sense when every user had a name and a login breaks when the most efficient users are non-human.

The companies figuring out their new pricing logic now -- while they still have runway and stable revenue to experiment -- will have a significant advantage over the ones who wait until the gap between what they charge and what they deliver becomes obvious to their customers.

The seat is dead. The question is what you're replacing it with.
