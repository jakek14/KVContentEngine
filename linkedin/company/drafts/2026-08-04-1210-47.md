## Post

97% of B2B website visitors leave without identifying themselves.

Most marketing teams already know that number. But there's a distinction underneath it that's changing how the best teams think about web traffic in 2026.

The gap between company-level and person-level visitor identification is larger than it sounds.

Company-level identification tells you: a company matching your ICP was on your pricing page yesterday.

Useful. You can route the domain to your sales team. Run account-based ads against the company. That's real intelligence.

Person-level identification tells you something different: a specific person with a specific title from that company visited your pricing page, spent seven minutes, came back the next day.

That's not just useful intelligence. That's a different category of signal.

One tells you a company might be evaluating you.

The other tells you exactly who to reach -- and gives you enough context to know roughly what they're trying to solve.

A few things that change when you know the person, not just the company:

Outreach relevance goes up significantly. You're not emailing a generic contact at the account hoping the right person opens it. You know who was actively engaged.

Prioritization gets sharper. A relevant title from a well-fit company visiting high-intent pages tells you more than a standard MQL form submission from someone still early in the research process.

Retargeting precision improves. Showing the right message to the person who was already on your site -- rather than all employees at the company -- changes the signal-to-noise ratio of your retargeting dramatically.

B2B buyers in 2026 are doing more independent research before they ever talk to a vendor. They read, compare, and evaluate without filling out a form. By the time they're ready to contact you, they've already decided a lot.

The teams who figure out person-level identification learn that intent exists earlier. Before the buyer signals it through a form.

That's the advantage worth building toward.
