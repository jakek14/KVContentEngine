## Post

AI startup deals average $51 million in 2026. Non-AI deals average $4.7 million.

That's a 10x gap. And it's reshaping how founders think about their own companies.

---

A lot of founders I talk to are quietly confused. They've built real businesses — users, revenue, retention. But every VC conversation ends with "we're focused on AI-native right now."

What's happening isn't that SaaS stopped working. The VC market bifurcated sharply into two different games. And many founders don't realize they've been sitting in the wrong room.

---

Investors now call this the "Precision Funding" era.

They're not looking for traction alone — they want what they call "Daily Active Profitability." Or at minimum, a mathematically defensible path to it.

Translation: growth without a clear path to margin is no longer fundable at any valuation that makes the math work. The days of losing money at scale and hoping growth covers it are over outside of the AI tier.

What that means for founders:

If you have a real business and you're staring at a $4.7M average non-AI check, the question to ask is whether that capital actually changes your trajectory — or whether you'd be giving up equity to accelerate something you could build to proof on your own first.

---

The founders who raise cleanly in 2026 share three things:

They know exactly what capital enables — not in principle, but in specific milestones and runway math.

They're raising from leverage, not from desperation. The company is already working. Capital speeds it up.

They can explain why outside capital is better than slower organic growth.

If you can't answer those three questions cold, the timing probably isn't right. And that's okay.

---

Building KnownVisitors, we've been intentional about staying lean while we find what's repeatable. Real capital is available when the case is clear. But the case has to be clear first.

The founders who walk into fundraising meetings knowing their number — and the story behind every component — are the ones having real conversations.

The ones who walk in hoping the narrative does the work usually discover it doesn't.
