## Post

The median SaaS CAC payback period just hit 18 months.

Three months longer than it was in 2023. Average customer acquisition cost is now $702 per customer acquired.

For a VC-backed company, 18-month payback is uncomfortable but manageable. You have runway. You're building toward a model that eventually works.

For a bootstrapped company, 18-month payback is a different problem entirely.

If it takes 18 months to recover the cost of acquiring a customer, you need to survive that window out of operating cash -- multiplied across every customer you're adding. The hole grows as you scale.

This is why bootstrapped founders think about unit economics differently from day one.

Not because they're smarter. Because the math leaves them no choice.

A few things that change when you're building without external capital:

You find customers where you can reach them cheaply. Communities, referrals, direct outreach, content that compounds over time. Channels where the cost doesn't reset to zero every month. You're not renting distribution -- you're building it.

You care about payback from the first acquisition. Not as a metric to report to investors. As a survival signal. Fast-payback channels get more investment. Slow ones get examined hard and cut if they don't tighten.

You over-invest in retention from month one. Churn is always expensive, but churn on a customer you haven't recovered the acquisition cost on yet is a direct cash loss. The first 90 days of the customer relationship get an outsized amount of attention.

You price to reflect actual value -- not aspirationally, not to feel competitive, but because underpricing creates the exact customers who churn fastest and expand least.

The 18-month payback problem isn't new for bootstrapped founders. The broader SaaS market is just catching up to economics that bootstrapped companies have been navigating for years.

The constraint was never a disadvantage. It was a forcing function.

The discipline it creates is the same discipline that makes bootstrapped companies disproportionately resilient when market conditions shift.
