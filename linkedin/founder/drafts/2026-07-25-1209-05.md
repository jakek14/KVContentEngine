## Post

The average startup now has 4.3 people.

That number gets treated like a data point. I think it's a signal.

For most of the last decade, team size correlated with output almost linearly. More people meant more code shipped, more campaigns running, more customer conversations happening. A 40-person team could just do more than a 4-person team -- not because they were smarter, but because they had more hours.

That math has changed.

The gap between what a small team can build and what a large team can build has closed faster than most people realize. Not because AI made work easier -- but because it changed which work actually requires people.

Marketing is a clear example. A few years ago, a proper marketing function needed a strategist, a copywriter, someone to run ads, a designer, and a data person to make sense of all of it. Five people minimum for a coherent program. Today a single person who knows how to work with AI tools can hold most of that stack and move faster than a siloed team where each person is waiting on the others.

Same story in product, in operations, in customer success.

The teams I see struggling aren't the small ones. They're the mid-sized ones who hired before they understood this -- who built a structure for a pre-AI execution environment and are now paying for it in salary, coordination costs, and speed.

4.3 people isn't a consolation prize for not raising a Series A. In 2026 it's increasingly a deliberate choice. And for companies that have figured out leverage, it's a structural advantage -- not a handicap.

The question I'd push founders to sit with: if AI lowered the cost of execution, where does your actual advantage live now? Because it's not in having more people. That edge is gone.

What's your current team size vs. what you're shipping? I'm curious whether more founders are thinking about this the same way.
