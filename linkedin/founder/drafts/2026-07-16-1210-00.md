## Post

The math on bootstrapping is better than most founders realize.

Bootstrapped companies reach $1M ARR about 4 months behind their venture-backed peers. Not a year. Not 3 years. Four months.

But they own 100% when they get there.

I've spent a lot of time with this number. The gap between those two outcomes -- 16 weeks vs. a cap table you'll spend the next decade renegotiating -- is the most underrated calculation in early-stage startups. Most people inside the VC machine talk about bootstrap like it's the consolation path. Like you chose it because you couldn't get funded.

That's not what the data shows.

Venture capital is more concentrated right now than at any point in the last 10 years. The dollars that used to spread across a wide range of early bets are clustering around AI mega-rounds and teams with elite pedigrees. For most founders building real software products, that window isn't just narrow -- it barely exists.

So a lot of people are bootstrapping by default. And discovering something the data already told them.

What's changed: the cost of bootstrapping dropped significantly while the expected returns stayed the same. AI tools compressed the time to early revenue. Infrastructure is cheaper. The go-to-market work that used to require a team of four can now be done by two people with the right stack.

Bootstrapped companies reaching $1M ARR are 90% likely to still be operating 10 years later. That's not a consolation stat. That's proof the model works.

The story I kept hearing when bootstrapping felt like settling -- "you'll grow slower, you'll have less resources, you'll lose to funded competitors" -- that story doesn't fit the data.

The real trade is 4 months for full ownership.

That's a trade worth making with clear eyes.
