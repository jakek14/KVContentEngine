## Post

There's a story making the rounds right now about a founder who turned down VC money.

His company just hit $50 million in revenue.

The reflex take is "bootstrapping wins." And maybe it did here. But the more interesting thing is why he turned down the capital in the first place.

Not because he was anti-VC on principle. He turned it down because taking it would have meant building a different product, on a different timeline, for different customers -- to hit numbers that made the investors' model work, not numbers that made his business work.

That's a different calculation than "outside money is bad."

I've talked to founders who took capital at the wrong moment. And what broke them wasn't the money itself. It was the operating model that came with it. Growth targets that required acquiring customers they couldn't retain. Hiring ahead of revenue that was supposed to appear. Spending against a forecast instead of against what was actually happening.

The founders who build something durable -- funded or not -- are the ones who hold onto the questions: who are we building for, and is what we're building actually working for them?

That question gets harder to answer when you have a board pushing for top-line growth at the expense of everything else.

We've been building KnownVisitors without outside capital. That means we get to answer that question every day. Not perfectly. But on our own terms.

The $50M story isn't an argument for bootstrapping. It's an argument for building something that doesn't require external pressure to make sense.
